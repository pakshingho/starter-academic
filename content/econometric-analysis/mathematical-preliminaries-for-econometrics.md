## 1.1 Matrix notation and useful results

We treat vectors as column vectors by default. A generic \(p \times 1\) vector is written as

$$
x =
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1 \\
x_2 \\
\vdots \\
x_p
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and the inner product of two vectors \(x\) and \(y\) is

$$
\langle x, y \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^p x_i y_i.
$$

A \(p \times q\) matrix \(A\) may be written as \(A = (a_{ij})\), with transpose

$$
A' = (a_{ji}).
$$

If \(A\) is \(p \times q\) and \(B\) is \(q \times r\), then the product \(AB\) is defined entrywise by

$$
(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^q a_{ik} b_{kj}.
$$

The key reminder is that matrix multiplication is generally not commutative:

$$
AB \neq BA.
$$

Two standard transpose identities that will recur later are

$$
(A + B)' = A' + B',
\qquad
(AB)' = B'A'.
$$

If \(A\) is square and \(|A| \neq 0\), then its inverse exists and satisfies

$$
AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I_p.
$$

For invertible conformable matrices,

$$
(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}.
$$

### Rank

The rank \(r(A)\) is the maximum number of linearly independent columns, equivalently rows, of \(A\).

Useful facts:

- If \(|A| \neq 0\) for a square matrix \(A\), then \(r(A) = p\).
- For conformable matrices, \(r(AB) \leq \min(r(A), r(B))\).

### Quadratic forms and positive definiteness

For a square matrix \(A\), the quadratic form generated by \(A\) is

$$
x'Ax = \sum_{i=1}^p \sum_{j=1}^p x_i a_{ij} x_j.
$$

We say:

- \(A\) is positive definite if \(x'Ax > 0\) for all \(x \neq 0\).
- \(A\) is positive semidefinite if \(x'Ax \geq 0\) and equality holds for at least one nonzero \(x\).

The ordering \(A > B\) means \(A - B\) is positive definite. When both inverses exist, \(A > B\) implies

$$
B^{-1} > A^{-1}.
$$

### Trace

The trace of a square matrix is

$$
\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^p a_{ii}.
$$

One especially useful identity is

$$
\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA).
$$

If \(r(A) = q \leq p\), then there exists a nonsingular matrix \(B\) such that

$$
BAB' =
\begin{pmatrix}
I_q & 0 \\
0 & 0
\end{pmatrix},
$$

or equivalently

$$
A = B^{-1}
\begin{pmatrix}
I_q & 0 \\
0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
(B')^{-1}.
$$

### Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

The eigenvalues of a square matrix \(A\) are the solutions \(\lambda_i\) to

$$
|A - \lambda I| = 0.
$$

For each eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\), there exists a nonzero vector \(x_i\) such that

$$
Ax_i = \lambda_i x_i.
$$

That vector \(x_i\) is an eigenvector. If \(\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p)\), then \(A\) can be written in diagonalized form as

$$
A = C \Lambda C'
$$

for some nonsingular matrix \(C\) whenever such a decomposition is available.

Useful consequences:

- If all eigenvalues are positive, \(A\) is positive definite.
- \(|A| = \prod_{i=1}^p \lambda_i\).
- \(\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^p \lambda_i\).
- The zeros of \(|A - B \lambda|\) are the eigenvalues of \(B^{-1}A\) when \(B\) is nonsingular.

### Matrix norms, symmetric matrices, and square roots

For a \(p \times q\) matrix \(A\), define the norm by

$$
\|A\|^2 = \lambda_{\max}(AA').
$$

If \(A\) is positive definite and invertible, then

$$
\|A^{-1}\| = \lambda_{\min}(A)^{-1}.
$$

If \(A\) is symmetric, then:

- all eigenvalues are real
- eigenvectors can be chosen to be real
- \(\lambda_{\max}(A) = \sup_x \frac{x'Ax}{x'x}\)
- \(\lambda_{\min}(A) = \inf_x \frac{x'Ax}{x'x}\)
- \(r(A)\) equals the number of nonzero eigenvalues

If \(A\) is positive definite, it has a square root \(B\) such that

$$
B^2 = A.
$$

Under spectral decomposition \(A = X' \Lambda X\), one convenient representation is

$$
B = X' \Lambda^{1/2} X.
$$

### Idempotent matrices

A matrix \(A\) is idempotent if

$$
A^2 = A.
$$

Two useful facts are:

- \(r(A) = \operatorname{tr}(A)\)
- the eigenvalues of \(A\) are only \(0\) or \(1\)

### Partitioned inverses and two core identities

For a partitioned matrix

$$
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
A_{11} & A_{12} \\
A_{21} & A_{22}
\end{pmatrix},
$$

if \(|A_{11}| \neq 0\), then the Schur complement of \(A_{11}\) is

$$
A_{22:1} = A_{22} - A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12},
$$

and the inverse can be written as

$$
A^{-1} =
\begin{pmatrix}
A_{11}^{-1}\!\left[I + A_{12}A_{22:1}^{-1}A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}\right] &
-A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}A_{22:1}^{-1} \\
-A_{22:1}^{-1}A_{21}A_{11}^{-1} &
A_{22:1}^{-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Two identities that become especially useful later in simultaneous-equation algebra are

$$
\operatorname{vec}(ABC) = (C' \otimes A)\operatorname{vec}(B)
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{tr}(ABCD) = \operatorname{vec}(C)' (D \otimes B') \operatorname{vec}(A').
$$

### 1.1.1 Matrix differentiation

For conformable vectors and matrices:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x'Ay) = Ay
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x'Ax) = (A + A')x.
$$

If \(A(\theta)\) depends on a parameter vector \(\theta\), one standard derivative identity is

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \ln |A_\theta|
=
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(A_\theta^{-1} \frac{\partial A_\theta}{\partial \theta}\right).
$$

For scalar \(\theta\),

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} A_\theta^{-1}
=
-A_\theta^{-1}
\left(\frac{\partial A_\theta}{\partial \theta}\right)
A_\theta^{-1}.
$$

The note also records the second-derivative formulas

$$
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2}\ln |A_\theta|
=
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(
-A_\theta^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial^2 A_\theta}{\partial \theta^2}\right)
-A_\theta^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial A_\theta'}{\partial \theta}\right)
A_\theta^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial A_\theta}{\partial \theta}\right)
\right)
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} A_\theta^{-1}
=
A_\theta^{-1}
\left(
\frac{\partial A_\theta}{\partial \theta}
A_\theta^{-1}
\frac{\partial A_\theta}{\partial \theta}
-
\frac{\partial^2 A_\theta}{\partial \theta^2}
\right)
A_\theta^{-1}.
$$

### 1.1.2 Inequalities

The note highlights four inequalities that appear repeatedly in probability and asymptotic arguments.

Cauchy-Schwarz:

$$
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i\right)^2
\leq
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\right)
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^2\right).
$$

Power-sum bound:

$$
|a+b|^p \leq C\left(|a|^p + |b|^p\right),
$$

where \(C\) depends only on \(p\).

Holder's inequality:

$$
\left|\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i\right|
\leq
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n |y_i|^q\right)^{1/q},
\qquad
\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = 1.
$$

More generally,

$$
\left|\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i z_i\right|
\leq
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n |y_i|^q\right)^{1/q}
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n |z_i|^r\right)^{1/r},
\qquad
\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} + \frac{1}{r} = 1.
$$

Minkowski:

$$
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i + y_i)^k\right)^{1/k}
\leq
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^k\right)^{1/k}
+
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^k\right)^{1/k},
\qquad
x_i, y_i \geq 0,\ k \geq 1.
$$

### Kronecker product

The Kronecker product of \(A\) and \(B\) is defined by

$$
A \otimes B = (a_{ij}B).
$$

Three useful properties are:

- \((A \otimes B)(C \otimes D) = AC \otimes BD\)
- \(r(A \otimes B) = r(A)r(B)\)
- if \(A\) is \(q \times q\) and \(B\) is \(p \times p\), then \(|A \otimes B| = |A|^p |B|^q\)
